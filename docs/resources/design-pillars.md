## UI Design Pillars

1\. Roster First — Make the Monday–Sunday weekly roster the primary workspace, with clear shift times, staff assignments, and uncovered slots.

2\. Clarity Over Complexity — Surface essential metrics—coverage, uncovered staff-hours, scheduled hours, and warnings—without overwhelming the manager.

3\. Direct, Responsive Editing — Enable quick assignment, reassignment, and removal, with immediate updates to staffing totals and coverage.

4\. Explainable Automation — Make auto-scheduling decisions understandable through visible demand requirements, staff availability, hour limits, and fairness considerations.

5\. Constraints With Clear Feedback — Prevent invalid assignments and explain why; flag policy exceptions with clear, actionable warnings.

6\. Honest Data Visualization — Distinguish required staffing from actual coverage, making shortages and overstaffing easy to identify at a glance.

7\. Trust Through Safe Changes — Validate imported data, confirm destructive actions, and protect manual edits during regeneration.

8\. Fairness Without Waste — Balance staff utilization while prioritizing demand coverage and respecting individual capacity.

Visual direction — Calm operational clarity

A clean, modern workforce-management interface. Prioritize structure, legibility, and information density over decoration. The product should feel dependable, efficient, and quietly intelligent.

Color palette

- Navy: primary text, navigation, strong hierarchy.
- Blue: primary actions, selected states, planned shifts.
- Teal: healthy coverage and positive outcomes.
- Amber: warnings and approaching limits.
- Red: uncovered demand, conflicts, critical errors.
- Cool gray: backgrounds, borders, secondary information.

Use semantic colors consistently; never rely on color alone to communicate status.

Typography and layout

Use a neutral sans-serif such as Inter. Keep headings compact, metrics prominent, and table labels highly legible. Align data precisely; use a consistent spacing scale, subtle borders, restrained corner radii, and minimal shadows.

Components and data visualization

Treat the weekly roster as the hero component. Use consistent shift cards, clear staff labels, compact coverage indicators, and aligned hourly demand charts. Keep required versus actual staffing visually distinct. Use icons only when they improve recognition.

Interaction and tone

Prefer inline editing, visible hover and focus states, concise confirmations, and actionable error messages. Use plain, neutral language: “2 hours uncovered” rather than vague alerts. Make system status and scheduling consequences explicit.

Design north star: A calm, data-driven scheduling workspace where managers can understand the week in seconds, fix gaps in minutes, and trust every automated recommendation.
